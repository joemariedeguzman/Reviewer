Case 12, Jamal Shamsie and Alan B. Eisner (2017) · Strategic management case analysis

Emirates Airline in 2017

Sustaining a Dubai-hub, premium-service growth model when transfer traffic, premium demand and yields are all under pressure.

Prepared by Joe Marie De Guzman. All financial figures in AED millions unless stated; fiscal years end 31 March.

Executive summary

Decision: Adopt Alternative 1, a full network integration of Emirates with its sister carrier flydubai at Dubai International (DXB), paired with a deferral of further Airbus A380 commitments in favour of the Boeing 777X.

  • Core problem: FY2016 profit rose 55% to AED 7,318m, but it was cost-led (operating costs fell AED 6.2bn on cheaper fuel) while revenue fell 3.7% and revenue per passenger fell 8.5%. Demand quality is eroding beneath record earnings.
  • Drivers: terrorism and regional conflict depress Gulf transfer traffic; oil-economy corporate travel cuts hit first and business class; Etihad, Qatar and Singapore Airlines are matching or exceeding Emirates' premium product; US carriers are attacking its legitimacy with subsidy claims; a large A380 fleet is hard to right-size.
  • Why flydubai integration wins: highest weighted-criteria score (4.60 of 5) and QSPM total (6.45 versus 5.57, 4.29 and 3.81); low capital need; same owner, so no ownership or regulatory barrier; adds new feeder markets; frees widebodies from thin routes; consistent with the IE Matrix "hold and maintain" prescription.
  • Implementation: three phases from April 2017 to 2020: commercial integration (codeshare, Skywards), network optimisation (route transfers, fleet deferral), and demand creation (Dubai stopover packages ahead of Expo 2020).
  • Contingency: pre-defined triggers activate capacity cuts (elements of Alternative 4) if transfer traffic keeps falling, or a premium economy launch (elements of Alternative 2) if yields do not stabilise.

The story of Emirates

A narrative retelling of the case, following its own sections.

Launching a dream

The roots of Emirates trace back to Gulf Air, a formidable airline jointly owned by the governments of Bahrain, Abu Dhabi, Qatar and Oman. In the early 1980s Gulf Air decided to cut flights into and out of Dubai. The young Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, upset by that decision, resolved to start an airline of his own, one that would help build Dubai into a centre of business and tourism, since the emirate lacked significant oil resources.

He recruited British Airways veteran Sir Maurice Flanagan to lay the groundwork and bankrolled the venture with US$10 million in royal funding. To lead it, he chose a member of the royal family, Sheikh Ahmed bin Saeed Al Maktoum, who at 26 had just graduated from the University of Denver and had never held a job. The young sheikh leaned on Flanagan to learn how to run an airline.

The airline's spectacular growth, however, is credited to Sir Tim Clark, who was handed route planning. Clark saw that about two-thirds of the world's population lived within eight hours' flight of Dubai, but the airline lacked aircraft that could exploit that location. That changed with the Boeing 777 in 1996 and the Airbus A380 in 2008, whose long range let Emirates link almost any two points on the globe with a single stop in Dubai. Clark called these "strange city pairs": New York and Shanghai, London and Nairobi. No airline had grown like this.

From 12 destinations in 1988, Emirates expanded at a remarkable pace, and it kept growing even through the 2008 recession, taking delivery of more new aircraft than any other carrier. "We operated normally. We put on more aircraft. We carried more passengers," said Mohammed H. Mattar, senior vice president of airport services. Along the way, Emirates helped redraw the map of world travel, shifting the hub of international traffic from Europe to the Middle East. Dubai now handles over 80 million passengers a year and is the world's busiest airport for international passengers, with a new US$4.5 billion terminal, the largest in the world, built to accommodate the almost 240 Emirates aircraft flying to 145 destinations.

Providing the ultimate experience

Emirates set out to give every passenger, in every cabin, the best possible experience. It was the first airline to put an entertainment screen in the back of every seat. "That seems pretty normal for long haul airlines now, but it wasn't then," said Terry Daly, the service manager, who is known for once firing eight service supervisors in a single day after discovering their crews had deviated from his precise instructions.

Food became a signature. The catering division, a multi-floor maze of monorails, cameras, wine warehouses, robotics and chefs of many nationalities, delivers 115,000 meal trays a day. "It's about making sure the culinary offering is absolutely first class across the airplane," Daly said.

The premium cabins went further still. Business class tickets include a chauffeured limousine to and from the airport, personal check-in assistance and access to 30 lounges, while a 600-strong multilingual welcome team called Marhaba, Arabic for "welcome", guides first- and business-class passengers through airport formalities. Emirates pioneered the first-class suite on the A340 in 2003; its 50 A380s each carry 14 suites with a vanity table, closet, 23-inch screen and sliding doors. First-class passengers can use two spa showers, an industry first. "To walk onto the A380, to have an average size bathroom, a seven minute shower, full size bath towels and your own attendant is pretty amazing," one event planner said. Premium passengers also share a horseshoe-shaped stand-up bar, for which Emirates gave up several business-class seats. Other carriers called such amenities frivolous; Emirates called them its edge.

Grooming a special employee

Behind the product is a carefully built workforce. Each year Emirates holds Open Days in more than 140 cities across 70 countries to recruit for an elite force of 18,000 flight attendants from 140 nationalities who speak more than 50 languages. Recruits are not drawn by the pay, about US$30,000 a year with free room and board, but by the chance to join an iconic global brand. Only about 5% of applicants make it through, in what the case likens to an American Idol-style brains-and-beauty contest.

Successful recruits spend seven weeks moving through departments in a training centre shaped like a jetliner fuselage, learning posture, etiquette, safety and evacuation. Standards are exacting, down to lipstick colour, hair shade and uniform; the crew is 75% women with an average age of 26, against over 40 at U.S. airlines. "When walking through an airport terminal, it's usually a Catch Me If You Can movie moment, with passengers all turning their heads," said one new recruit. Training ends with motivational exercises called Nujoum, Arabic for stars. Travel writer Christine Negroni, who took part, described it as "a combination of a customer service experience and a come-to-Jesus rally", leaving recruits asking, "What can I do for Emirates?"

Communicating to the masses

Despite all this, focus groups revealed that the Emirates name was not well known in many of the markets it was entering. The airline needed a message that would tell consumers what to expect and rally employees behind its promise. True to form, it summoned the world's top 10 advertising agencies to Dubai to compete for the account.

The winner was StrawberryFrog, a young New York agency. Its founder, Scott Goodson, had read an interview in which Tim Clark said he wanted Emirates to make the world a smaller place by bringing people together. That became "Hello Tomorrow", not merely a slogan but a way of thinking about the airline as a catalyst for a global culture of shared aspirations. "Ad campaigns are fleeting. The power of a movement is that it can change habits and rally millions," Goodson told Clark. StrawberryFrog spent 18 months at headquarters turning employees into foot soldiers of the movement before the brand launched in spring 2012 in 150 countries. In one television ad, a steward pushes his drink cart as an A380 is built around him from parts and people drawn from across the globe.

Chasing tomorrow

By 2017 the story had reached a turning point. Falling oil prices and rising terrorism were cutting demand. Companies, especially in the Middle East, were trimming employee travel, reducing the premium revenue Emirates depended on, and fearful passengers were avoiding connections through the region. Emirates swapped some A380 routes to smaller 777s, cut back on additional A380 orders until traffic recovered, and tried to attract tourists to Dubai itself.

The largest U.S. airlines accused Emirates, Etihad and Qatar of receiving government subsidies. Clark insisted Emirates had never received subsidies or cheap fuel, pointing to 27 straight years of audited profits: "We are confident that any allegation that Emirates has been subsidized is totally without grounds."

Rivals were also catching up. Singapore Airlines launched an enhanced premium economy cabin with wider seats, cocktail tables and noise-cancelling headsets. Next door in Abu Dhabi, Etihad unveiled The Residence, a three-room suite at US$21,000 one way, and US$16,000 First Apartments, complete with Savoy-trained butlers, improving on the very suite concept Emirates had pioneered 12 years earlier.

Some observers began to wonder whether the airline's best days were behind it. "I could make the case that Emirates' moment has passed," business travel writer Joe Brancatelli said. "Emirates was the trendy airline three or four years ago." Chairman and CEO Sheikh Ahmed brushed the concern aside: "Over the years, we have always managed to come up with new products." Whether new products alone would be enough is the question this analysis sets out to answer.

3Analysis of the external environment

Conclusion: The industry environment has turned from tailwind to headwind. Cheap fuel is the only major external factor still working in Emirates' favour, and it is temporary. The hub-and-transfer model is exposed to security, political and technological threats at the same time.

Problem statement

How can Emirates restore revenue quality and sustain profitable growth of its Dubai-hub network over FY2017 to FY2020, given declining transfer and premium demand, intensifying premium competition from Etihad, Qatar and Singapore Airlines, political pressure on its traffic rights, and a fleet sized for continuous high-density growth?

Objectives

  • Arrest the decline in revenue per passenger and return it to growth by FY2019.
  • Increase connecting traffic through DXB from new source markets to offset losses on Middle East-sensitive flows.
  • Keep an operating margin of at least 8% even if fuel prices normalise.
  • Protect liquidity: keep cash and deposits above AED 15bn and improve the current ratio toward 0.9.
  • Preserve the premium brand and service culture that underpin the sustained advantages identified in the VRIO analysis.

7Alternative courses of action

Mutual exclusivity rule: each alternative is a single primary strategic thrust for FY2017 to FY2020 and commits the same constrained resources (about AED 10bn a year of capital spending, senior management bandwidth and the fleet plan) in a different direction. Choosing one rules out the others as the primary strategy.

Alternative 1: Dubai hub network integration with flydubai Recommended

Strategy type: market penetration through related-carrier integration (organic, low capital).

  • Sign a comprehensive partnership with flydubai, the Dubai-based low-cost carrier owned by the same shareholder (Investment Corporation of Dubai): full codeshare across both networks, coordinated schedules at DXB, through-checked baggage and joint Emirates Skywards earn-and-burn.
  • flydubai's Boeing 737 network feeds secondary cities in the Gulf, Africa, Central Asia, the Indian subcontinent and Eastern Europe into Emirates long-haul flights.
  • Transfer thin or seasonal Emirates routes to flydubai; redeploy Emirates widebodies to dense trunk routes.
  • Defer additional Airbus A380 commitments and prioritise the Boeing 777X order for future growth, so capacity can follow demand.

Advantages

  • Adds new source markets without buying aircraft
  • Raises load factors on A380s and 777s
  • No ownership, antitrust or foreign-approval barrier (same owner)
  • Fast to implement (6–12 months for codeshare)
  • Diversifies the transfer base away from Middle East-sensitive flows

Disadvantages

  • Does not directly fix premium product gaps
  • Brand dilution risk if low-cost legs feel inconsistent with Emirates service
  • Still concentrated on one hub (DXB)
  • IT and operational integration complexity

Why exclusive: it commits the network plan to a single-hub DXB model and a fleet deferral; it is incompatible with a dual-hub merger (Alternative 3) and with deep retrenchment (Alternative 4), and it reallocates capital away from a fleet-wide cabin retrofit (Alternative 2).

Alternative 2: Premium product reboot

Strategy type: product development and differentiation (high capital).

  • Introduce a premium economy cabin across the A380 and 777 fleet to compete directly with Singapore Airlines' new premium economy.
  • Launch fully enclosed, next-generation first-class private suites on new Boeing 777-300ERs, co-designed with Mercedes-Benz design teams, to answer Etihad's The Residence and First Apartments.
  • Upgrade the 30-lounge network and expand the Marhaba service.

Advantages

  • Directly targets yield erosion and the product gap
  • Leverages the service culture (sustained advantage)
  • Re-establishes innovation leadership

Disadvantages

  • Very heavy capex and retrofit downtime
  • Bets on premium demand just as corporate travel is being cut
  • Amenities are imitable (parity, per VRIO)
  • Does not address falling transfer traffic

Why exclusive: the fleet-wide retrofit absorbs the capital and aircraft downtime that the other alternatives would deploy elsewhere.

Alternative 3: UAE consolidation through a merger with Etihad Airways

Strategy type: horizontal integration (inorganic).

  • Propose to the federal UAE government a merger with Etihad Airways (Abu Dhabi), combining fleets (221 + 102 aircraft) and networks, and running DXB and Abu Dhabi as a coordinated dual hub.
  • Eliminate fare competition between two UAE hubs, rationalise duplicated routes and strengthen bargaining power with Airbus and Boeing.

Advantages

  • Removes the closest premium rival (Etihad's Residence product comes in-house)
  • Scale and purchasing power
  • Rationalised overlapping capacity

Disadvantages

  • Political complexity between Dubai and Abu Dhabi ownership
  • Absorbs Etihad's weaker finances and its loss-making equity stakes in other airlines
  • Long timeline; cultural integration risk
  • Would intensify U.S. and EU subsidy and competition scrutiny

Why exclusive: a merger would consume management attention for years and replace the single-hub DXB model with a dual-hub structure.

Alternative 4: Strategic retrenchment and capacity discipline

Strategy type: defensive retrenchment.

  • Cancel or defer remaining A380 deliveries with Airbus, retire older aircraft early and park surplus A380s.
  • Cut frequencies on weak U.S. and Middle East-sensitive routes; freeze cabin crew hiring and Open Days.
  • Use savings to reduce borrowings and raise the current ratio.

Advantages

  • Fast cost relief; protects liquidity
  • Reduces exposure to U.S. political pressure
  • Low execution risk

Disadvantages

  • Surrenders scale and network reach to Qatar and Etihad
  • Confirms the "Emirates' moment has passed" narrative
  • Weakens Dubai's hub economy, the airline's founding purpose
  • Damages the Airbus relationship and residual A380 values

Why exclusive: shrinking capacity contradicts the growth and investment logic of the other three alternatives.

9Recommendation

Emirates should integrate its network with flydubai at DXB under a single commercial partnership, and rebalance its fleet plan from further A380 commitments toward the Boeing 777X, so that it can fill its existing widebodies from new source markets and grow capacity in step with demand.

  • Addresses the problem: new feed from secondary markets offsets transfer traffic lost on Middle East-sensitive flows and raises load factors, which lifts revenue per flight even while premium demand is soft.
  • Builds on sustained advantages: the DXB hub, network design capability (Tim Clark's route planning) and the brand, all rated sustained advantages in the VRIO analysis.
  • Protects the balance sheet: requires mainly commercial and IT investment rather than aircraft, preserving free cash flow (AED 4.2bn in FY2016) and liquidity.
  • Reduces political exposure: growth comes from Africa, Asia and Eastern Europe rather than from more capacity into the U.S., lowering the profile of the subsidy dispute.
  • Supporting moves (within the same thrust): reinforce financial transparency in public communications (audited results, no fuel subsidies) and package Dubai stopovers with Dubai Tourism and Emirates Holidays to convert transfer passengers into visitors.
