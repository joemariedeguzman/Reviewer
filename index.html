Executive summary

Decision

Adopt Alternative 1: integrate the Emirates network with sister carrier flydubai at Dubai International (DXB), and defer further Airbus A380 commitments in favour of the Boeing 777X.

  • Core problem: FY2016 profit rose 55% to AED 7,318M, but the gain was cost-led (operating costs fell AED 6.2bn on cheaper fuel) while revenue fell 3.7% and revenue per passenger fell 8.5%. Demand quality is eroding beneath record earnings.
  • Drivers: terrorism and regional conflict depress Gulf transfer traffic; oil-economy corporate travel cuts hit first and business class; Etihad, Qatar and Singapore Airlines are matching or exceeding Emirates' premium product; U.S. carriers allege subsidies; a large A380 fleet is hard to right-size.
  • Why flydubai integration wins: highest weighted-criteria score (4.60 of 5) and QSPM total (6.45 versus 5.57, 4.29 and 3.81); low capital need; same owner, so no ownership or regulatory barrier; adds feeder markets; frees widebodies from thin routes; matches the IE Matrix "hold and maintain" prescription.
  • Implementation: three phases from April 2017 to 2020: commercial integration, network optimisation, and demand creation ahead of Expo 2020 Dubai.
  • Contingency: pre-defined triggers activate capacity cuts (elements of Alternative 4) if transfer traffic keeps falling, or a premium economy launch (elements of Alternative 2) if yields do not stabilise.

The story of Emirates

In brief

A small start-up founded in 1985 to put Dubai on the map became one of the world's biggest international carriers in three decades. By 2017 the conditions behind that rise were weakening, and critics were asking whether its moment had passed.

Central conflict

An airline built on growth, transfer traffic and premium luxury now faces falling premium demand, travellers avoiding the region, and rivals copying its signature products. Can "new products" alone carry it into tomorrow?

Launching a dream

The roots of Emirates trace back to Gulf Air, a formidable airline jointly owned by the governments of Bahrain, Abu Dhabi, Qatar and Oman. In the early 1980s Gulf Air decided to cut flights into and out of Dubai. The young Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, upset by that decision, resolved to start an airline of his own, one that would help build Dubai into a centre of business and tourism, since the emirate lacked significant oil resources.

He recruited British Airways veteran Sir Maurice Flanagan to lay the groundwork and bankrolled the venture with US$10 million in royal funding. To lead it, he chose a member of the royal family, Sheikh Ahmed bin Saeed Al Maktoum, who at 26 had just graduated from the University of Denver and had never held a job. The young sheikh leaned on Flanagan to learn how to run an airline.

The airline's spectacular growth, however, is credited to Sir Tim Clark, who was handed route planning. Clark saw that about two-thirds of the world's population lived within eight hours' flight of Dubai, but the airline lacked aircraft that could exploit that location. That changed with the Boeing 777 in 1996 and the Airbus A380 in 2008, whose long range let Emirates link almost any two points on the globe with a single stop in Dubai. Clark called these "strange city pairs": New York and Shanghai, London and Nairobi. No airline had grown like this.

From 12 destinations in 1988, Emirates expanded at a remarkable pace, and it kept growing even through the 2008 recession, taking delivery of more new aircraft than any other carrier. "We operated normally. We put on more aircraft. We carried more passengers," said Mohammed H. Mattar, senior vice president of airport services. Along the way, Emirates helped shift the hub of international travel from Europe to the Middle East. Dubai now handles over 80 million passengers a year and is the world's busiest airport for international passengers, with a new US$4.5 billion terminal, the largest in the world, built to accommodate the almost 240 Emirates aircraft flying to 145 destinations.

Providing the ultimate experience

Emirates set out to give every passenger, in every cabin, the best possible experience. It was the first airline to put an entertainment screen in the back of every seat. "That seems pretty normal for long haul airlines now, but it wasn't then," said Terry Daly, the service manager, who is known for once firing eight service supervisors in a single day after discovering their crews had deviated from his precise instructions.

Food became a signature. The catering division, a multi-floor maze of monorails, cameras, wine warehouses, robotics and chefs of many nationalities, delivers 115,000 meal trays a day. "It's about making sure the culinary offering is absolutely first class across the airplane," Daly said.

The premium cabins went further still. Business class tickets include a chauffeured limousine to and from the airport, personal check-in assistance and access to 30 lounges, while a 600-strong multilingual welcome team called Marhaba, Arabic for "welcome", guides first- and business-class passengers through airport formalities. Emirates pioneered the first-class suite on the A340 in 2003; its 50 A380s each carry 14 suites with a vanity table, closet, 23-inch screen and sliding doors. First-class passengers can use two spa showers, an industry first. "To walk onto the A380, to have an average size bathroom, a seven minute shower, full size bath towels and your own attendant is pretty amazing," one event planner said. Premium passengers also share a horseshoe-shaped stand-up bar, for which Emirates gave up several business-class seats. Other carriers called such amenities frivolous; Emirates called them its edge.

Grooming a special employee

Behind the product is a carefully built workforce. Each year Emirates holds Open Days in more than 140 cities across 70 countries to recruit for an elite force of 18,000 flight attendants from 140 nationalities who speak more than 50 languages. Recruits are not drawn by the pay, about US$30,000 a year with free room and board, but by the chance to join an iconic global brand. Only about 5% of applicants make it through, in what the case likens to an American Idol-style brains-and-beauty contest.

Successful recruits spend seven weeks moving through departments in a training centre shaped like a jetliner fuselage, learning posture, etiquette, safety and evacuation. Standards are exacting, down to lipstick colour, hair shade and uniform; the crew is 75% women with an average age of 26, against over 40 at U.S. airlines. "When walking through an airport terminal, it's usually a Catch Me If You Can movie moment, with passengers all turning their heads," said one new recruit. Training ends with motivational exercises called Nujoum, Arabic for stars. Travel writer Christine Negroni, who took part, described it as "a combination of a customer service experience and a come-to-Jesus rally", leaving recruits asking, "What can I do for Emirates?"

Communicating to the masses

Despite all this, focus groups revealed that the Emirates name was not well known in many of the markets it was entering. The airline needed a message that would tell consumers what to expect and rally employees behind its promise. True to form, it summoned the world's top 10 advertising agencies to Dubai to compete for the account.

The winner was StrawberryFrog, a young New York agency. Its founder, Scott Goodson, had read an interview in which Tim Clark said he wanted Emirates to make the world a smaller place by bringing people together. That became "Hello Tomorrow", not merely a slogan but a way of thinking about the airline as a catalyst for a global culture of shared aspirations. "Ad campaigns are fleeting. The power of a movement is that it can change habits and rally millions," Goodson told Clark. StrawberryFrog spent 18 months at headquarters turning employees into foot soldiers of the movement before the brand launched in spring 2012 in 150 countries. In one television ad, a steward pushes his drink cart as an A380 is built around him from parts and people drawn from across the globe.

Chasing tomorrow

By 2017 the story had reached a turning point. Falling oil prices and rising terrorism were cutting demand. Companies, especially in the Middle East, were trimming employee travel, reducing the premium revenue Emirates depended on, and fearful passengers were avoiding connections through the region. Emirates swapped some A380 routes to smaller 777s, cut back on additional A380 orders until traffic recovered, and tried to attract tourists to Dubai itself.

The largest U.S. airlines accused Emirates, Etihad and Qatar of receiving government subsidies. Clark insisted Emirates had never received subsidies or cheap fuel, pointing to 27 straight years of audited profits: "We are confident that any allegation that Emirates has been subsidized is totally without grounds."

Rivals were also catching up. Singapore Airlines launched an enhanced premium economy cabin with wider seats, cocktail tables and noise-cancelling headsets. Next door in Abu Dhabi, Etihad unveiled The Residence, a three-room suite at US$21,000 one way, and US$16,000 First Apartments, complete with Savoy-trained butlers, improving on the very suite concept Emirates had pioneered 12 years earlier.

Some observers began to wonder whether the airline's best days were behind it. "I could make the case that Emirates' moment has passed," business travel writer Joe Brancatelli said. Chairman and CEO Sheikh Ahmed brushed the concern aside: "Over the years, we have always managed to come up with new products." Whether new products alone would be enough is the question this analysis sets out to answer.

Emirates carried 51.9 million passengers in FY2016 and earned a record AED 8,330M operating profit, yet revenue fell 3.7% as premium and transfer demand weakened.

Analysis: growth is steady, profit is not
  • Traffic grew every single year: passengers rose 4.1 times, from 12.5M to 51.9M, a compound growth rate of about 13.8% a year, including through the 2008–2009 recession.
  • Profit swings sharply with fuel and the economy: it fell 48.8% in FY2009 (AED 4,451M to 2,278M) and 66.7% in FY2012 (AED 5,443M to 1,813M), even though passengers kept rising.
  • Profit per passenger has not kept pace: about AED 161 per passenger in FY2016 versus AED 209 in FY2005. The record profit comes from scale, not from earning more on each traveller.
  • Implication: FY2016's record should not be read as a return to strength; history shows profit can halve in a year when fuel or demand turns.

Analysis: a cost-led profit jump
  • Revenue fell AED 3,228M (−3.7%) and other operating income fell AED 547M (−26.2%), a combined drop of AED 3,775M.
  • Operating costs fell AED 6,212M (−7.5%), more than offsetting the revenue drop. Costs consumed 91.9% of revenue, down from 95.6%. The case attributes the period to falling oil prices, so fuel is the most likely driver.
  • Operating profit rose AED 2,437M (+41.4%), which is exactly the cost saving minus the income lost. None of the improvement came from higher sales.
  • Financing costs eased: net finance costs fell from AED 1,274M to 1,109M (−13%), helped by more finance income.
  • Tax is negligible: income tax of AED 45M is about 0.6% of pre-tax profit, reflecting the low-tax UAE base, a point U.S. rivals use in their competitive-advantage arguments.
  • Implication: if fuel prices rise again, the margin gain reverses unless revenue per passenger recovers.

Analysis: a capital-heavy, leveraged but liquid balance sheet
  • Asset base grew 7.0% to AED 119,179M while revenue fell, so each dirham of assets generated less revenue.
  • Aircraft dominate: property, plant and equipment is 69.5% of total assets, typical of an airline and the reason fleet decisions drive returns.
  • Shift toward leasing: advance lease rentals nearly tripled (AED 920M to 2,580M) as Emirates sold aircraft (AED 6,535M of proceeds in the cash flow) and leased capacity instead, lightening ownership of the fleet.
  • Collections slowed: trade receivables rose 8.5% while revenue fell; days sales outstanding rose from about 36 to 41 days.
  • Strong cash buffer: cash rose 44.9% to AED 12,165M; with short-term deposits, liquid funds of AED 19,988M equal 16.8% of total assets.

  • Negative working capital is structural: current liabilities exceed current assets by AED 7,097M, largely because airlines hold large trade payables and customer advances; manageable given the cash buffer.

Analysis: what the ratios say
  • Profitability improved sharply (operating margin 10.0%, net margin 8.8%, ROE 22.6%), but on the cost side only.
  • DuPont view: ROE of 22.6% = net margin 8.8% × asset turnover 0.70 × equity multiplier 3.68. In FY2015 it was 16.7% = 5.5% × 0.78 × 3.94. The entire ROE gain came from margin; asset efficiency and leverage both declined.
  • Efficiency is the warning sign: asset turnover fell from 0.78× to 0.70× and revenue per passenger fell 8.5%. Emirates is putting more aircraft and capital to work for less revenue.
  • Liquidity is tight but backed by cash: a current ratio of 0.82 is normal for airlines, and AED 20.0bn of cash and deposits covers more than half of current liabilities.
  • Solvency is sound and improving: debt to equity fell from 1.69× to 1.55× and interest coverage rose from 4.1× to 6.3×.
  • Overall: Emirates is financially healthy enough to fund a response, but its earnings depend on fuel prices; the strategic task is to rebuild revenue quality before that cushion disappears.

Internal environment

In brief

The hub, service culture and brand are sustained advantages, and the balance sheet can fund a response. The weak points are revenue quality and an asset base built for a growth environment that has stalled.

Financial analysis

Full statements and ratios are in the Case facts tab.

  • Profit is cost-led, not demand-led: operating costs fell AED 6.2bn (−7.5%) while revenue fell AED 3.2bn (−3.7%), lifting operating margin from 6.8% to 10.0%.

Problem analysis & statement

Problem statement

How can Emirates restore revenue quality and sustain profitable growth of its Dubai-hub network over FY2017 to FY2020, given declining transfer and premium demand, intensifying premium competition from Etihad, Qatar and Singapore Airlines, political pressure on its traffic rights, and a fleet sized for continuous high-density growth?

Objectives

  • Arrest the decline in revenue per passenger and return it to growth by FY2019.
  • Grow connecting traffic from new source markets to offset Middle East-sensitive flows.
  • Keep an operating margin of at least 8% at normalised fuel prices.
  • Keep cash and deposits above AED 15bn; move the current ratio toward 0.9.
  • Preserve the brand and service culture behind the sustained advantages.

Alternative courses of action

Mutual exclusivity

Each alternative is a single primary thrust for FY2017 to FY2020 and commits the same constrained resources (about AED 10bn a year of capex, management bandwidth and the fleet plan) in a different direction. Choosing one rules out the others.

Alternative 1: Dubai hub network integration with flydubai Recommended

Sign a comprehensive partnership with flydubai, the Dubai low-cost carrier owned by the same shareholder (Investment Corporation of Dubai): full codeshare, coordinated DXB schedules, through-checked baggage and joint Emirates Skywards. flydubai's Boeing 737 network feeds secondary cities in the Gulf, Africa, Central Asia, the Indian subcontinent and Eastern Europe into Emirates long-haul flights. Thin routes move to flydubai; Emirates widebodies redeploy to dense trunks; further A380 commitments are deferred in favour of the Boeing 777X.

Pros

  • New feed without new aircraft: adds source markets and lifts load factors on A380s and 777s.
  • No ownership barrier: same owner, so no antitrust or foreign-approval hurdle.
  • Speed: codeshare achievable in 6–12 months.
  • Diversification: broadens transfer sources beyond Middle East-sensitive flows.

Cons

  • Product gap remains: does not directly answer Etihad and Singapore cabins.
  • Brand dilution risk: low-cost legs may feel inconsistent with Emirates service.
  • Still single-hub: concentration at DXB persists.

Exclusive because it commits to a single-hub DXB model and a fleet deferral, incompatible with a dual-hub merger or deep retrenchment, and it diverts capital from a fleet-wide retrofit.

Alternative 2: Premium product reboot

Introduce a premium economy cabin across the A380 and 777 fleet to compete with Singapore Airlines; launch fully enclosed next-generation first-class suites on new Boeing 777-300ERs, co-designed with Mercedes-Benz, to answer Etihad's The Residence; upgrade the 30 lounges and expand Marhaba.

Pros

  • Targets yield directly: captures upscale mid-priced demand.
  • Leverages service culture: a sustained advantage.
  • Restores innovation leadership.

Cons

  • Heavy capex and retrofit downtime.
  • Mistimed: bets on premium demand as corporate travel is cut.
  • Imitable: amenities are competitive parity under VRIO; does not fix transfer losses.

Exclusive because the fleet-wide retrofit absorbs the capital and aircraft time the other options need.

Alternative 3: UAE consolidation through a merger with Etihad Airways

Propose to the UAE federal government a merger with Etihad Airways (Abu Dhabi), combining fleets (221 + 102 aircraft) and networks and running DXB and Abu Dhabi as a coordinated dual hub.

Pros

  • Removes the closest premium rival.
  • Scale: stronger bargaining with Airbus and Boeing; rationalised overlapping routes.

Cons

  • Political complexity between Dubai and Abu Dhabi ownership.
  • Absorbs Etihad's weaker finances and its loss-making stakes in other airlines.
  • Intensifies U.S. and EU subsidy and competition scrutiny; long timeline.

Exclusive because a merger consumes years of management attention and replaces the single-hub model.

Alternative 4: Strategic retrenchment and capacity discipline

Cancel or defer remaining A380 deliveries with Airbus, retire older aircraft early, park surplus A380s, cut frequencies on weak U.S. and Middle East-sensitive routes, freeze crew hiring, and use savings to reduce borrowings.

Pros

  • Fast cost relief; protects liquidity.
  • Lower U.S. political exposure; low execution risk.

Cons

  • Surrenders scale to Qatar and Etihad.
  • Confirms the "moment has passed" narrative; weakens Dubai's hub economy.
  • Damages the Airbus relationship and A380 residual values.

Exclusive because shrinking capacity contradicts the growth logic of the other three.

  • Alt 1 (selected): best balance of root-cause fit, low capital and speed; builds on sustained advantages.

Recommendation

Recommendation

Integrate the Emirates network with flydubai at DXB under a single commercial partnership, and rebalance the fleet plan from further A380 commitments toward the Boeing 777X, so Emirates fills existing widebodies from new source markets and grows capacity in step with demand.

  • Addresses the problem: new secondary-market feed offsets transfer traffic lost on Middle East-sensitive flows and lifts load factors, supporting revenue per flight while premium demand is soft.
  • Builds on sustained advantages: the DXB hub, Clark's network design capability and the brand.
  • Protects the balance sheet: mainly commercial and IT investment, preserving free cash flow (AED 4.2bn in FY2016).
  • Reduces political exposure: growth comes from Africa, Asia and Eastern Europe rather than more U.S. capacity.
  • Supporting moves within the same thrust: reinforce financial transparency (audited results, no fuel subsidies) and package Dubai stopovers with Dubai Tourism and Emirates Holidays.

Timeline

Four phases from April 2017 to 2020, governed by a joint Emirates–flydubai steering committee.
