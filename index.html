Case background

In brief

BlackBerry held about 20.1% of the global smartphone market in 2009, then lost its position to the iPhone and Android. By 2017 it had moved away from making phones and was rebuilding around enterprise software, security, QNX and licensing.

Company background and competitive decline

  • Research in Motion (RIM), founded in 1984 in Waterloo, Ontario, became a leading smartphone company through its BlackBerry devices, known for secure communications, wireless email, and physical keyboards.
  • Its strong relationships with corporate and government customers established BlackBerry as a trusted brand in mobile communications. At its peak, BlackBerry held approximately 20.1% of the global smartphone market in 2009.
  • Apple's iPhone and the rapid growth of Android changed the basis of competition. Consumers increasingly preferred touchscreen devices, wider application availability, and integrated digital ecosystems; BlackBerry's advantages in security, email, and physical keyboards became insufficient.
  • Unsuccessful product launches, delays in developing the BlackBerry 10 operating system, and internal leadership disagreements weakened its competitiveness.
  • The growing adoption of Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) policies reduced enterprise dependence on BlackBerry devices and its proprietary network.

Existing capabilities and resources

  • Security expertise and brand equity: BlackBerry maintained a reputation for secure communications, supported by its established relationships with enterprise and government customers.
  • Enterprise software and services: The company had developed capabilities in enterprise mobility management, secure communications, and related software solutions.
  • QNX technology: BlackBerry owned an embedded operating system with applications in automotive systems and other connected technologies.
  • Patents and intellectual property: Its portfolio of patents and proprietary technologies provided opportunities for licensing, partnerships, and commercialization.
  • Financial resources: The company retained cash and other financial assets. In April 2017, BlackBerry also secured an $815 million arbitration award against Qualcomm, providing additional financial flexibility.

These resources represented potential sources of competitive advantage, although their ability to generate sustainable growth and profitability remained uncertain.

Management response and strategic position

  • John Chen became CEO in November 2013 and initiated restructuring: reducing operating costs, limiting exposure to smartphone hardware manufacturing, and expanding focus on enterprise software, secure communications, technology solutions, and licensing.
  • By 2017, BlackBerry had moved away from directly manufacturing smartphones and was pursuing a business model less dependent on hardware sales, while facing established technology companies and emerging enterprise software and security providers.
  • Although its software business was growing, BlackBerry had not yet demonstrated that its remaining businesses could consistently replace lost revenues and deliver sustainable profitability.

Problem analysis & statement

Problem statement

How should BlackBerry leverage its existing capabilities and resources to achieve sustainable growth and profitability amid increasing competition and changing industry dynamics?

Root cause analysis

BlackBerry's decline was not caused by a single unsuccessful product but by its failure to adapt its business model and competitive capabilities to fundamental changes in the smartphone industry.

Root causeCase evidenceBusiness impact
1. Failure to anticipate market shiftsManagement underestimated the shift toward touchscreen smartphones, mobile applications, and consumer-driven purchasing preferences.BlackBerry's traditional product advantages became less relevant, resulting in declining market share.
2. Weak innovation and product executionThe BlackBerry Storm, Torch, and BB10 failed to establish a competitive position against iOS and Android. Product delays and software limitations weakened execution.BlackBerry struggled to retain customers and recover lost hardware sales.
3. Dependence on a proprietary ecosystemBlackBerry relied heavily on its devices, operating system, and network infrastructure. The rise of BYOD reduced enterprise dependence on this closed ecosystem.Declining device sales and system access fees eroded traditional revenue sources.
4. Leadership and strategic misalignmentDisagreements between senior leaders contributed to delayed decisions and inconsistent responses to industry changes.BlackBerry lost valuable time while competitors strengthened their market positions.
5. Slow replacement of declining revenuesDespite Software & Services revenue growing by 161.8% from FY2015 to FY2017, total segment revenue declined by 58.8%.Emerging businesses had not achieved sufficient scale to replace traditional revenues and restore profitability.

Synthesis

  • BlackBerry's decline was primarily driven by its inability to respond effectively to changing customer preferences and technological developments, compounded by weak product execution, dependence on its proprietary ecosystem, and leadership disagreements that delayed strategic decisions.
  • By 2017, management had begun addressing these weaknesses through restructuring and diversification, but software and services growth remained insufficient to replace declining traditional revenues.
  • The strategic challenge had shifted from defending the smartphone business to determining how remaining technological capabilities, intellectual property, customer relationships, and financial resources could generate sustainable growth and profitability.

SWOT analysis

In brief

BlackBerry's strengths lie in trust, security, patents and a growing software line. Its weaknesses are uncompetitive hardware, collapsing system access fees, heavy losses and small scale against security incumbents.

Strengths

  • Trusted security brand with governments and enterprises
  • Growing software/services line ($249M to $652M, FY2015 to FY2017, +161.8%)
  • Patent portfolio with proven licensing value
  • $815 million Qualcomm award; cost base cut under Chen

Weaknesses

  • Non-competitive hardware and no consumer ecosystem
  • Collapsing System Access Fees ($1,606M to $313M, −80.5%) and heavy losses ($1,206M net loss, FY2017)
  • Small scale versus Symantec, Microsoft, and Verizon
  • History of slow decisions and internal leadership conflict

Opportunities

  • Growth in enterprise mobility management and cybersecurity spending
  • Licensing the BlackBerry brand and name to OEMs (asset-light hardware), IoT
  • Embedded/automotive software (QNX heritage)
  • Secure communications niches; regulatory and privacy tailwinds favoring certified secure vendors

Threats

  • Entrenched enterprise-security incumbents (Symantec, Verizon, Imperva)
  • Continued erosion of SAF as carriers decommission BlackBerry services
  • Platform owners (Apple, Google) absorbing security features natively
  • Any re-entry into consumer hardware invites commoditization by Chinese vendors

TOWS matrix

In brief

The matrix points toward software, security and licensing, funded by disciplined cost control and away from capital-intensive hardware.

SO strategies (use strengths to seize opportunities)

  • Leverage security expertise and enterprise relationships to expand software solutions.
  • Commercialize patents through licensing.
  • Explore growth opportunities in QNX and connected technologies.

WO strategies (overcome weaknesses using opportunities)

  • Accelerate replacement of declining traditional revenues through software expansion, partnerships, and selective acquisitions.
  • Redeploy resources toward businesses with stronger growth potential.

ST strategies (use strengths to counter threats)

  • Differentiate through trusted security capabilities.
  • Use strategic partnerships and licensing to compete without significant hardware investment.

WT strategies (minimize weaknesses and avoid threats)

  • Rationalize underperforming operations and control operating costs.
  • Preserve liquidity while reducing exposure to highly competitive, capital-intensive businesses.

Alternative courses of action

In brief

Four alternatives were considered, differing in where BlackBerry's revenue would come from: enterprise customers, embedded technology and IP, a sale or merger, or device manufacturers.

Alternative 1: Expand as an enterprise software and security company Recommended

Concentrate resources on becoming a major provider of enterprise software, cybersecurity, and secure communications solutions, accelerating organic development and pursuing selective acquisitions to expand the portfolio, customer base, and recurring software revenues.

Pros

  • Leverages existing strengths: builds on security expertise, enterprise customer relationships, and the established software portfolio.
  • Recurring revenue potential: subscriptions and enterprise contracts could provide more predictable revenues and higher margins than hardware.
  • Established growth momentum: Software & Services revenue increased by 161.8% between FY2015 and FY2017.

Cons

  • Intense competition: established enterprise software and cybersecurity providers have greater scale and resources.
  • High investment requirements: product development, acquisitions, and customer acquisition would require substantial financial commitments.
  • Concentration risk: heavy dependence on enterprise software increases exposure if the business fails to achieve sufficient market penetration.

Alternative 2: Focus on embedded technology and IP licensing

Reposition primarily as a technology and IP provider concentrating on QNX, automotive applications, connected devices, and patent licensing, commercializing proprietary technologies through OEM agreements, licensing arrangements, and strategic partnerships.

Pros

  • Leverages proprietary technology: QNX and the patent portfolio provide opportunities beyond the smartphone market.
  • Lower capital intensity: licensing and embedded software could generate revenues without large-scale hardware manufacturing.
  • Growth opportunities: adoption of connected vehicles and IoT could expand demand for embedded software.

Cons

  • Dependence on OEM adoption: growth depends on manufacturers selecting and integrating BlackBerry technologies.
  • Long commercialization cycles: automotive and embedded systems require extensive development, testing, and qualification.
  • Uncertain revenue scale: licensing income and royalties may not grow sufficiently or quickly enough to replace declining revenues.

Alternative 3: Pursue a strategic sale or merger

Pursue a sale or merger with a larger technology, enterprise software, or cybersecurity company able to integrate BlackBerry's security technologies, IP, and customer relationships, maximizing shareholder value through a negotiated transaction rather than an independent turnaround.

Pros

  • Potential shareholder value realization: immediate value and reduced exposure to future turnaround risks.
  • Access to greater resources: a larger company could provide capital, distribution, and organizational capabilities.
  • Operational synergies: combining technologies and eliminating duplicated functions could improve cost efficiency.

Cons

  • Loss of independence: surrenders control over future strategic direction and long-term upside.
  • Valuation risk: declining performance could weaken the negotiating position and result in an unattractive price.
  • Execution and regulatory risks: regulatory scrutiny, integration challenges, and uncertain buyer interest.

Alternative 4: Become a security technology supplier to global device manufacturers

License and integrate security technologies, encrypted communications, and enterprise device management into third-party platforms such as Samsung and potentially Apple, expanding the existing Samsung collaboration while pursuing additional OEM customers.

Pros

  • Leverages security expertise: capitalizes on reputation and proprietary security technologies without returning to smartphone manufacturing.
  • Access to established device ecosystems: larger installed bases and global distribution channels.
  • Recurring licensing opportunities: licensing, integration services, and enterprise security agreements across multiple platforms.

Cons

  • Strong OEM bargaining power: Apple and Samsung may demand unfavorable commercial terms.
  • Limited differentiation: major manufacturers already invest heavily in proprietary security.
  • Partner dependence: growth depends on OEM adoption and partnership continuity.

Evaluation results

  • Alt 1 (4.60): strong strategic fit built on enterprise security capabilities, customer relationships, and a growing software business; substantial investment, but an opportunity for recurring revenues and sustainable profitability.
  • Alt 2 (4.00): leverages QNX and IP with lower investment; growth depends on licensing agreements and the pace of adoption in automotive and embedded markets.

Decision & implementation plan

Decision

Reposition BlackBerry as an enterprise software and security company, with profitability and cash generation taking priority over market share expansion. Implement over FY2018 to FY2020 with phased, milestone-based funding.

Recommendation

  • Alternative 1 gives BlackBerry the strongest opportunity to return to sustainable growth and profitability by building on its strengths in enterprise security, software, and customer relationships, rather than attempting to regain its position in smartphones.
  • Although Alternative 3 offers the highest estimated value under the financial assumptions, Alternative 1 lets BlackBerry retain ownership of its technologies and participate in future growth, with greater alignment to its capabilities and long-term direction.
  • Prioritize enterprise software subscriptions, stronger security solutions, and existing corporate customers to generate recurring revenues. Phase investments and tie them to measurable improvements in revenue growth, operating profitability, and cash flow.

Implementation roadmap

  • FY2018 Q1–Q2 · CEO, CFO, Product ManagementEvaluate and prioritize the software portfolio. Assess products on revenue, margins, demand, and competitive strength; prioritize enterprise mobility management, endpoint security, and secure communications; identify products for investment, consolidation, or discontinuation. Outcome: resources concentrated on software businesses with the strongest commercial potential.
  • FY2018 Q3–Q4 · CTO, Product ManagementStrengthen product competitiveness. Improve integration across enterprise solutions; ensure compatibility with Android, iOS, and other environments; develop subscription packages by customer size, security requirements, and service level; test with existing customers before broader rollout. Outcome: more competitive solutions that address enterprise security needs beyond BlackBerry devices.
  • FY2019 Q1–Q2 · Enterprise SalesExpand within the existing customer base. Identify enterprise and government accounts for additional subscriptions; prioritize renewals, cross-selling, and migration of legacy customers; assign account-level revenue and retention targets. Outcome: higher recurring revenues from established relationships.
  • FY2019 Q3–Q4 · Sales, Business DevelopmentAcquire new enterprise customers. Target financial services, healthcare, and government; strengthen partnerships with resellers, systems integrators, and managed-service providers; focus on customers securing multiple devices and operating systems. Outcome: broader customer base and reduced dependence on legacy customers.
  • FY2020 · CEO, CFO, Product ManagementScale profitable operations. Increase investment in products with strong growth, retention, and margins; reduce spending on underperformers; improve onboarding, support, and renewals to control cost-to-serve. Outcome: improved operating profitability and sustainable cash generation.

Key implementation considerations

  • Prioritize enterprise security rather than compete across the entire cybersecurity market. Build on enterprise mobility management, secure communications, and device security; expanding into unrelated segments would require new capabilities against more established competitors.
  • Make BlackBerry software independent of BlackBerry hardware. Customers must be able to use BlackBerry security across different devices and operating systems, given widespread Android and iOS adoption in corporate environments.
  • Use existing customers as the initial source of growth. Identify customers already using BlackBerry software, assess their additional security needs, and offer relevant solutions through cross-selling and subscription upgrades.
  • Manage the decline of legacy businesses without weakening the software business. Keep cutting hardware and legacy service costs, but protect software engineering, security expertise, and customer support; report software performance separately from legacy operations.

Contingency plan

If Alternative 1 underperforms

Shift to Alternative 2: Embedded Technology & IP Licensing, ranked second in the weighted scoring model.

  • Conduct a formal strategic review at the end of FY2019 to assess whether enterprise software expansion is generating sufficient returns.
  • If performance remains below expectations, gradually redirect investment toward commercializing QNX, expanding licensing with automotive and embedded-device manufacturers, and monetizing the IP portfolio.
  • Make the transition selective: retain profitable enterprise software operations while reducing investment in underperforming products.
