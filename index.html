Executive summary

Decision

Adopt Alternative 1: narrow the portfolio by spinning off Justice as a separate public company, folding Catherines into Lane Bryant and closing loss-making dressbarn stores, then use the cash to pay down the debt taken on for ANN.

  • Core problem: Ascena grew to ten brands and 4,906 stores mostly by buying other retailers. Comparable sales are now falling in every segment (total −4% in Q2 FY2017), and the company is carrying US$1,532 million of long-term debt from the ANN deal while its tangible equity is negative.
  • What is driving it: mall traffic is shrinking, younger shoppers are spending on experiences, discounters and online players are taking share, and Ascena is late to omni-channel. Ten brands also spread management time and cash thinly.
  • Why focus wins: it has the highest weighted score (4.05 of 5), the highest QSPM total (6.69) and the highest illustrative value (about US$2.8 billion). It attacks the debt and the complexity at the same time, and it follows the Grand Strategy Matrix, which places Ascena in Quadrant IV (retrenchment and divestiture).
  • Implementation: March 2017 to FY2020 in four phases: prepare the separation, complete the Justice spin-off and dressbarn closures, focus on the core brands, then scale omni-channel.
  • Contingency: if the spin-off cannot be completed on good terms, sell Justice to a buyer instead; if sales keep sliding, move to Alternative 2 (deeper store closures across all brands).

The story of Ascena

In brief

A small dress shop for working women grew into the largest U.S. women’s specialty retailer by buying one chain after another. By 2017, with ten brands, 4,900 stores and heavy debt, analysts were asking whether it could survive.

Ascena’s story starts in 1962, when there were few work clothes for women entering the workforce. Roslyn Jaffe and her husband Elliot opened the first Dress Barn in Stamford, Connecticut. The idea worked: by 1982 the company went public on NASDAQ as DBRN, and by 1985 it had 200 stores across the U.S. aimed at working women aged 35 to 55. In 1989 it added Dress Barn Woman for plus-size customers.

In the 1990s, office dress became more casual, so Dress Barn added sportswear, shoes, petites and jewelry. Elliot and Roslyn’s son David became CEO in 2002. Growth then came mainly through acquisitions. In 2005 Dress Barn bought maurices, a Duluth, Minnesota chain for women aged 17 to 34 in small-town strip malls, known for sizes 0 to 26 and in-store “stylists”. In 2009 it bought Justice, a tween chain for girls aged 7 to 14 that had once belonged to The Limited.

In 2011 the company reorganised as Ascena Retail Group (NASDAQ: ASNA). A year later it bought Charming Shoppes, adding the plus-size brands Lane Bryant and Catherines, followed by the Cacique and Catherines Intimates lines. Then came the biggest move of all. In August 2015 Ascena bought ANN INC., the owner of Ann Taylor, LOFT and Lou & Grey, for US$47 a share: US$37.34 in cash plus 0.68 of an Ascena share. Ascena became the largest U.S. specialty retailer focused only on women and girls, with ten brands in four segments, more than 4,900 stores and projected sales of over US$7 billion.

The timing was difficult. ANN itself had been struggling since 2014, with weak products at Ann Taylor, heavy discounting, missed earnings, slow inventory turnover and activist investors pushing for change. Ascena hoped to save US$150 million a year by combining ANN’s sourcing, procurement, distribution and logistics with its own. But its older brands had shown disappointing same-store sales for years, and most of its growth had come from buying companies rather than growing them.

By 2017 the retail climate had turned hostile. Ten retailers filed for bankruptcy in the first quarter alone, and nineteen more were on a “distressed” list. Department stores and specialty chains were closing stores. Ascena’s comparable sales fell in every segment in the second quarter of FY2017, and analysts warned that the company needed most of its cash flow just to pay down debt. Ascena’s COO Brian Lynch described “a paradigm shift in retail”. The question at the end of the case is blunt: what are Ascena’s odds of survival?

We analyse the case from the point of view of David Jaffe, President and CEO of Ascena Retail Group, in March 2017, just after the second-quarter FY2017 results. Jaffe turned his parents’ Dress Barn into the largest women’s specialty retailer in the U.S., largely by buying other chains, the most recent and most expensive being ANN INC. Now he has to answer the question analysts are asking out loud: did Ascena overplay its hand? He needs a plan that brings sales back, protects the company’s ability to service its debt, and gives Ascena a real chance of surviving one of the worst years in specialty retail since 2009.

The Specialty Apparel Industry

Apparel retailers fall into three groups: discount mass merchandisers (Target, Walmart, TJX, Costco), multi-tier department stores (Macy’s, JCPenney, Kohl’s, Nordstrom) and specialty chains such as Ascena. Specialty retailers focus on one type of product for one type of customer, which makes them more exposed when that customer changes. To spread the risk they keep adding brands for new niches, but that creates branding problems of its own.

The case gives several warnings. Gap spent US$40 million on Forth & Towne for women over 35 and closed it after 18 months. Limited Brands spent decades buying and selling brands (Abercrombie & Fitch, Limited Too, Lane Bryant, Lerner, Express) before renaming itself L Brands and focusing on Victoria’s Secret and Bath & Body Works, a choice that worked. Talbots and Coldwater Creek both failed chasing the “misses” customer and ended up owned by private equity, while Chico’s FAS, with a strong balance sheet and little debt, became the one clear winner in that segment.

In 2017 L Brands, with US$12 billion of sales and five brands, was the second-largest U.S. specialty apparel retailer behind Gap (also five brands) and just ahead of Ascena, which had ten.

Ascena Operations

Ascena invested more than US$300 million between FY2013 and FY2016 to build a shared services platform: consolidated corporate functions, global sourcing, a distribution and fulfilment network and an omni-channel platform. CEO David Jaffe described the goal as turning “seven $1 billion companies into ONE $7 billion powerhouse” through centres of excellence in procurement, sourcing, real estate, digital, supply chain and analytics.

The store fleet is large and mostly in older formats: 2,178 stores in strip centres, 1,463 in enclosed malls, 708 in outlets and 557 in lifestyle centres and downtown locations (Exhibit 1 in the Annex). Management admitted it “probably” had too many stores and started a “fleet optimization project”. Online, only ANN, Justice and maurices had full e-commerce platforms; the other brands were due to launch theirs in FY2017.

Financial Performance

Thanks to ANN, total net sales jumped from US$4,802.9 million in FY2015 to US$6,995.4 million in FY2016 (Annex Exhibit A). Take ANN out and the picture changes: the rest of the business brought in about US$4,664.5 million, roughly 2.9% less than the year before. The case text quotes US$3,562 million for sales without ANN, which does not match Exhibit 2; we use the exhibit figures. Operating income recovered from a US$234.9 million loss in FY2015, when Lane Bryant took a US$308 million impairment, to a US$93.8 million profit in FY2016, an operating margin of only 1.3%.

FY2017 started badly. In the second quarter, net sales fell 5.1% to US$1,748.2 million, comparable sales fell 4% and Ascena lost US$35.2 million. For the first six months, the net loss was US$20.8 million. Gross margin improved, but higher selling, occupancy and restructuring costs ate the gain. Long-term debt stood at US$1,532.0 million, and goodwill and intangibles from the acquisitions (US$2,549.3 million) were larger than total equity (US$1,859.5 million).

Existing Capabilities and Resources

Ascena still had real assets to work with:

  • Scale: the largest U.S. women’s specialty retailer, about US$7 billion in sales and 4,906 stores.
  • A broad portfolio: brands covering women and girls of different ages, sizes and incomes, including strong niche positions in plus sizes (Lane Bryant, Catherines) and small-town fashion (maurices).
  • Shared services: a US$300 million+ platform for sourcing, distribution and corporate functions, plus US$150 million in targeted ANN synergies.
  • Improving gross margin: 57.2% for the first half of FY2017, up from 53.2%.
  • Customer relationships: stylists at maurices and personal client service at Ann Taylor.

The question is whether these assets can carry ten brands and a heavy debt load through a shrinking market.

Management Response and Strategic Position

Management’s plan was to keep all ten brands and make them work better together: finish integrating ANN, roll out e-commerce to every brand, add buy-online-pick-up-in-store and one-click checkout, and trim the store fleet. COO Brian Lynch argued that Ascena’s scale and shared services gave it capabilities that were “better, faster and more cost-efficient than our competitors”.

Analysts were less convinced. One noted that “the company needs much of the cash flow to pay down the debt and the balance sheet is too levered to provide the margin of safety.” Others pointed out that the debt could make Ascena a takeover target. The central challenge is how Ascena can survive the downturn and return to profitable growth with the portfolio, stores and balance sheet it has.

Key Findings

Economic and Social factors put the most pressure on Ascena. Shoppers are spending less on clothes and less time in malls, and when they do buy, they expect to move easily between phone, website and store. Technology is both the threat and the way out. Legal factors matter mainly because leases slow down store closures.

Strategic implication: the market will not rescue Ascena. Growth has to come from fewer, stronger brands and better omni-channel selling, not from more stores or more acquisitions.

Profitability

Gross margin is the one bright spot. It rose from 53.2% to 57.2% over the first half, partly because the year-earlier figure included about US$140.6 million of non-cash inventory charges from the ANN purchase. The problem sits below the gross margin. Sales fell 5.1% in Q2 while selling, general and administrative costs only fell 2.1%, so SG&A rose to 30.8% of sales. Add US$20.2 million of restructuring charges and higher depreciation, and the Q2 operating margin fell to −2.6%. For the half year, Ascena barely broke even at the operating line (US$6.5 million) and still lost US$20.8 million after interest.

Liquidity

The current ratio held steady at 1.22, so Ascena is not in immediate trouble paying its bills. But the quality of its liquidity is slipping. Cash fell from US$371.8 million to US$299.5 million while inventory rose to US$676.1 million, pushing the quick ratio down from 0.58 to 0.51. Rising inventory while sales fall usually means more markdowns ahead.

Leverage and Solvency

Ascena paid down about US$116.5 million of debt during the half year, lowering debt-to-equity from 0.88 to 0.82. That is progress, but the bigger issue is what sits behind the equity. Goodwill and intangibles total US$2,549.3 million, nearly half of all assets and more than the company’s entire equity. Strip them out and tangible equity is about −US$690 million. If the acquired brands keep underperforming, impairment charges could wipe out much of the book equity, as the US$308 million Lane Bryant charge did in FY2015.

Debt Service

Interest costs about US$50 million every six months. Operating income of US$6.5 million covered only 0.13 times that. Including non-cash depreciation, EBITDA covered interest 3.9 times, which keeps the company afloat but leaves little room. Even on Ascena’s own adjusted figures, coverage halved from 3.55 to 1.77 times. This is what analysts meant when they said the balance sheet had no margin of safety.

Brand Performance

The brands are moving in very different directions (Annex Exhibits A and F). maurices grew 13.4% from FY2014 to FY2016 and earned a 9.6% operating margin in FY2016, by far the best in the group. Justice shrank 20.1% over the same two years. dressbarn lost money in FY2016 and Value Fashion lost US$19.8 million in Q2. Premium Fashion was the only segment to improve its quarterly operating income, from −US$5.8 million to US$22.7 million, even though Ann Taylor’s comparable sales fell 9%.

Overall Assessment

Ascena’s finances show a company that can pay its bills today but is running out of room. Gross margins and debt repayment are moving the right way. Sales, operating margins, interest coverage and cash are moving the wrong way, and the balance sheet depends on the value of acquired brands that are not all performing. Any strategy has to raise operating profit and cut debt at the same time, without needing much new money.

Problem analysis & statement

Problem statement

How can Ascena restore profitable growth and protect its ability to service the debt from the ANN acquisition, given falling comparable sales in every segment, an oversized store fleet in declining retail formats, and a ten-brand portfolio that spreads management attention and capital too thin?

Synthesis

Ascena’s core problem is that it bought its way to scale at the moment scale stopped protecting retailers. Each acquisition added sales, but also more brands to run, more stores to pay for and, with ANN, a large amount of debt. When mall traffic fell and shoppers moved online, all of these weaknesses showed up together: comparable sales dropped everywhere, costs did not fall as fast as sales, and operating income stopped covering interest.

Management’s answer so far is to keep every brand and run them more efficiently through shared services. That helps at the margin, but it does not fix the main issue, which is a portfolio too wide and a balance sheet too stretched for the market Ascena now faces.

Objectives

  • Return comparable sales to flat by FY2019.
  • Reach an operating margin of at least 4% by FY2020.
  • Repay at least US$500 million of debt by the end of FY2018.
  • Bring operating income to at least two times interest expense.
  • Have every remaining brand selling online, with buy-online-pick-up-in-store, by FY2019.

Alternative courses of action

Mutual exclusivity

Each alternative below is a different main direction for FY2017 to FY2020. They all compete for the same limited cash, borrowing capacity and management attention, so choosing one rules the others out as the main strategy.

Alternative 1: Focus the Portfolio and Pay Down Debt

Ascena would spin off Justice as a separate public company, the way Limited Brands spun off Abercrombie & Fitch in 1996. Before the spin-off, Justice would raise its own debt and pay a one-time dividend to Ascena, which Ascena would use to repay its term loan. Catherines would be folded into Lane Bryant’s merchandising and store operations, and about 250 loss-making dressbarn stores would close as their leases expire. Ascena would end up with a women-only portfolio built around ANN, maurices and Lane Bryant.

Pros:

  • Tackles Debt and Complexity Together: cuts debt by about US$500 million and removes the brand furthest from Ascena’s women’s core.
  • Keeps Value for Shareholders: they receive shares in Justice instead of a fire-sale price.
  • Follows What Worked: mirrors L Brands, which succeeded after narrowing its portfolio.

Cons:

  • Smaller Company: revenue falls by about a fifth, and fixed shared-service costs must be cut to match.
  • Execution Risk: a spin-off needs separate systems, financing and a public listing, which can take more than a year.
  • Justice Debt Capacity: a shrinking Justice may not support enough debt to fund a large dividend.

Alternative 2: Keep All Ten Brands, Shrink the Fleet and Go Omni-channel

Ascena would keep its full portfolio and accelerate its own plan. It would close about 400 underperforming stores across all brands, mostly in enclosed malls, and roll out e-commerce, buy-online-pick-up-in-store and one-click checkout to dressbarn, Lane Bryant and Catherines on the Salesforce Commerce Cloud platform. Savings from closures would fund the technology.

Pros:

  • Builds on the Current Plan: management has already started fleet optimisation and omni-channel investment.
  • Keeps Scale: retains all ten brands and their customers.
  • Lower Execution Risk: no separation or transaction needed.

Cons:

  • Complexity Stays: ten brands still compete for limited money and attention.
  • Slow Debt Reduction: cash from savings is shared between debt and new investment.
  • Weak Brands Keep Draining Resources: Justice and dressbarn remain inside the group.

Alternative 3: Sell the Company to Sycamore Partners

Ascena would seek a buyer for the whole company. The case notes that its debt and large store base could make it a takeover target. A natural candidate is Sycamore Partners, the private equity firm that bought Talbots and Coldwater Creek, which would take Ascena private, refinance the debt and restructure the brands away from public markets.

Pros:

  • Immediate Value: shareholders receive cash now, avoiding the risk of a failed turnaround.
  • Access to Capital: a private owner can restructure without quarterly pressure.
  • Speed of Restructuring: private equity can close stores and sell brands quickly.

Cons:

  • Weak Negotiating Position: falling sales and high debt would depress the price.
  • Loss of Independence: the Jaffe family and shareholders give up any upside.
  • Buyer Uncertainty: there is no guarantee a buyer will pay an acceptable price.

Alternative 4: Invest in Premium Fashion Growth

Ascena would make the ANN brands its growth engine. It would reset Ann Taylor’s product and pricing to end heavy discounting, open about 100 stand-alone Lou & Grey stores in lifestyle centres and expand LOFT online, funding this with new borrowing and cash from the Value Fashion segment.

Pros:

  • Builds on the Improving Segment: Premium Fashion was the only segment to raise Q2 operating income.
  • Higher-income Customers: ANN customers are more affluent and shop in downtown and lifestyle locations.
  • Trend Fit: Lou & Grey taps into casual and active wear.

Cons:

  • More Debt: adds borrowing to an already stretched balance sheet.
  • Ann Taylor Still Struggling: comparable sales fell 9% in Q2.
  • Ignores the Rest of the Portfolio: problems at Justice and dressbarn remain untouched.

Alternative 1: Focus the Portfolio ranked first, with a weighted score of 4.05, a QSPM total of 6.69 and the highest estimated value. It deals with debt and complexity together and keeps the brands that actually have an edge.

Recommendation

Recommendation

Spin off Justice, fold Catherines into Lane Bryant, close loss-making dressbarn stores and use the cash to repay about US$500 million of debt.

We recommend Alternative 1: Focus the Portfolio and Pay Down Debt. It scored 4.05 out of 5.00 on the Weighted Scoring Model, had the highest QSPM total of 6.69 and produced the highest estimated value.

This option goes straight at the two things most likely to sink Ascena: too much debt and too many brands. Spinning off Justice removes a shrinking business that serves a completely different customer, and the dividend Justice pays before the spin-off cuts Ascena’s debt by about US$500 million. Folding Catherines into Lane Bryant and closing loss-making dressbarn stores removes duplicated costs and stores that lose money. What is left is a women’s retailer built around its strongest positions: maurices, the plus-size business and the ANN brands.

Selling the company would give shareholders certainty, but at a weak price set at the bottom of the cycle. Keeping all ten brands is safer to carry out, but it leaves Ascena running the same complex portfolio with the same debt in a market that is still shrinking. Our recommendation keeps the upside with Ascena’s shareholders, who also keep their stake in Justice.

In practice, Ascena should prepare the spin-off and the dressbarn closures first, then turn the savings into omni-channel capabilities for the remaining brands and a product reset at Ann Taylor. Spending should be released in stages and tied to clear results in debt reduction, comparable sales and operating margin.

In short, Ascena should become smaller, simpler and less indebted so that it can survive the downturn and grow again from a stronger base.

Implementation plan

Timeline

March 2017 to FY2020 in four phases: prepare, separate and simplify, cut debt, then strengthen and scale the core brands.

We propose carrying out the plan from March 2017 through FY2020. Ascena’s fiscal year ends in late July, so FY2018 runs from August 2017 to July 2018. The work has three priorities: separate Justice and cut debt, simplify the women’s portfolio and store fleet, and rebuild sales in the remaining brands.

The order matters. Debt has to come down first, because every dollar of interest saved is a dollar that can go into the remaining brands. Store closures should follow lease expiry dates to avoid costly early exits.

Key Implementation Considerations

1. Pay down debt before investing in growth.

The Justice dividend and closure savings should go to the term loan first. Only once interest coverage improves should Ascena spend more on growth.

2. Treat the spin-off as a fresh start for Justice.

Justice needs its own management, systems and enough financing to compete. A weak spin-off that fails would hurt Ascena’s reputation and the value shareholders receive.

3. Protect the plus-size customer during the Catherines integration.

Catherines customers need extended sizes that Lane Bryant does not fully cover. The brand can live on as a line inside Lane Bryant stores and online, so the merger cuts costs without losing these customers.

4. Close stores by lease date and move customers online.

When a dressbarn store closes, Ascena should point its customers to the nearest remaining store and to dressbarn.com, so that the sales are kept wherever possible.

Contingency plan

If Alternative 1 underperforms

If the spin-off cannot be financed, sell Justice instead; if sales keep falling, close more stores across all brands (Alternative 2); if covenants come under pressure, open sale talks (Alternative 3).

If Alternative 1 cannot be carried out as planned, Ascena should turn to Alternative 2: Keep All Brands, Shrink the Fleet and Go Omni-channel, which ranked second in the Weighted Scoring Model.

The most likely problem is the spin-off itself. If markets will not support a separate Justice listing on acceptable terms by the end of the first half of FY2018, Ascena should sell Justice to a strategic or private equity buyer instead and still use the proceeds to repay debt.

If comparable sales keep falling by more than 5% after the separation, or interest coverage does not improve, Ascena should move to deeper store closures across all brands, as in Alternative 2.
